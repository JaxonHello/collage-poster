Create an ultra-wide 12:5 experimental editorial key visual based on:

[SCENE OR SUBJECT DESCRIPTION]

a character,

Automatically determine the strongest visual concept, title hierarchy, environmental motifs, symbolic imagery, color palette and visual rhythm from the subject.

TITLE SYSTEM:
Do not simply use the literal scene description as the headline.

Create one short, evocative, high-impact editorial title based on the theme, conflict, emotion or symbolism of the scene.

The main title should feel like:
a chapter title,
an exhibition title,
an album title,
a fashion editorial title,
or a cinematic conceptual phrase.

Keep the main title concise, ideally 1–4 words.

Avoid low-level descriptive titles such as:
“Luffy vs Kaido”
“Battle of X”
“Character A Fighting Character B”

Instead, infer a more conceptual title from the scene.

Use:
1 oversized primary title,
1 short secondary title,
2–4 micro annotation groups,
and optionally a small franchise / series label.

If a franchise or series is recognizable from the subject, keep its name small and secondary.

Exact important text should be readable.
Avoid random pseudo-English and long paragraphs.

VISUAL STYLE:
Japanese contemporary graphic design × Swiss editorial typography × dense deconstructed collage.

Create ONE SINGLE DENSE INTERLOCKED COLLAGE CLUSTER concentrated primarily in the central 55–65% of the canvas.

About 70–85% of all visual information should be compressed into this main cluster.

The collage must feel compact, interconnected and deliberately art-directed.

Image blocks should:
touch,
overlap,
interlock,
crop into one another,
share alignment lines,
and partially obscure neighboring elements.

Use narrow gutters.

Avoid isolated floating rectangles,
loose mosaics,
gallery-style spacing,
even card layouts,
or scattered decorative fragments.

Use approximately:
3–5 large anchor image blocks,
5–9 medium image fragments,
several small embedded details,
2–4 large solid color fields,
and a few long structural bars.

Medium and small elements should fill gaps between larger blocks.

Strong horizontal visual flow across the entire 12:5 canvas.
Allow major elements to enter and exit both edges.

Keep negative space mainly around the outer perimeter,
not between the central collage modules.

SUBJECT COMPOSITION:
Automatically choose the best visual hierarchy for the scene.

For a single-character subject:
use one dominant character plus 2–4 secondary close-up fragments.

For a multi-character confrontation:
allow two major figures to share visual dominance,
but avoid simple left-versus-right symmetry.

Use overlap, scale contrast, diagonal tension, partial cropping,
environmental separation and graphic geometry to create conflict.

For narrative scenes:
prioritize the emotional moment over literal depiction.

For symbolic or atmospheric subjects:
allow architecture, objects, weather, silhouettes or typography
to become major visual anchors.

Characters should break through the modular grid
rather than sit neatly inside isolated frames.

GRAPHIC LANGUAGE:

Graphic elements should organize and connect the composition,
not randomly decorate empty space.

COLOR SYSTEM:
Automatically infer a sophisticated 4–5 color palette from the subject.

Use:
one dominant thematic color,
one secondary color,
near-black / charcoal,
warm off-white,
and one restrained accent color.

Keep roughly 45–60% of the overall canvas light or off-white,
unless the narrative strongly benefits from a darker atmosphere.

Use a few large color masses rather than many unrelated small color squares.

VISUAL HIERARCHY:
Prioritize:

1. narrative focal point
2. main title
3. dominant character / characters / symbolic subject
4. major environmental imagery
5. large color fields
6. supporting close-ups or fragments
7. technical graphics
8. micro typography

At thumbnail size,
the central narrative and main title should still be immediately clear.
